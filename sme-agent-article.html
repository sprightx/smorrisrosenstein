The most valuable agent on my AI marketing team is the one that's allowed to say no

The dangerous AI-generated copy isn't the marketing hype. Hype is easy to spot, and you can ban “revolutionary” with a word list. The dangerous copy is the stuff that sounds right because you're not a subject matter expert, but will instantly stand out to your buyers who are.

I market software to pharmaceutical manufacturers. My buyers are heads of Quality, process engineers and operations leaders. They all have STEM master's degrees and some of them have PhDs. A sentence that looks right to me might set off alarm bells to them and tell them I have no idea what I'm talking about.

When I moved most of my content production into Claude Code, I built a team of agents: a product marketer, a content writer, an ad copywriter, a demand-gen strategist and a performance analyst. The agent that delivers the most value is none of those. It's the subject matter expert, and nothing customer-facing ships without its verdict.

A reviewer, not a writer

The subject matter expert agent is defined in its own file, and the persona is deliberately specific. It has a chemical engineering background, 15+ years in Quality, Process Engineering and Operations at pharma manufacturers and contract manufacturers, and now advises manufacturers on operational strategy. It has sat in the buyer's chair.

The most important lines in that file say what it does not do. It doesn't rewrite for tone, judge headlines, assess campaign strategy or give marketing feedback of any kind. Its single question is whether each claim would survive scrutiny from a VP of Quality or a Director of Process Engineering.

Giving it a narrow scope is what makes it useful. A reviewer allowed to comment on everything turns into a second copywriter, its domain flags get lost among style suggestions, and the writers learn to ignore it.

What it checks

The reviewer works through three passes:

  1. Technical claim audit. It lists every factual claim, metric, regulatory reference and competitive comparison. Is each one accurate? Does it trace to our approved proof-point library? Is it attached to the right product?
  2. Credibility screen. It reads the whole piece as the target buyer would and flags anything that would trigger skepticism: inflated claims, misused terminology, oversimplified regulation, pain points that don't match the reader's actual job.
  3. Competitive fairness. Would a buyer who also evaluated the competitor call our comparison honest?

It applies 13 named review lenses, written in the language of the domain rather than marketing. They cover batch variability, critical process parameter (CPP) and critical quality attribute (CQA) methodology, regulatory positioning, and calibrating technical depth to the persona.

The output has a fixed format:

  • Every flag carries a severity, the exact text, why a buyer would object, and a suggested revision.
  • RED means it's wrong or materially overstated and must be fixed before anything ships.
  • YELLOW means it isn't wrong, but a professional would find it imprecise or off.
  • The verdict is APPROVED, APPROVED WITH REVISIONS or REQUIRES REWORK.

The writing agent fixes only what was flagged, and the expert re-reviews only those items.

There's also one governance rule I'd keep in any version of this: marketing reasoning can't overrule a RED flag. If the expert says a claim fails, it gets revised or removed. Arguing it into submission isn't an option.

What it actually catches

Here are some real flags, lightly anonymised:

  • “Calculations run in the record, so a transcription error cannot carry into the next step.” Overstated. Running calculations inline removes arithmetic errors, but an operator who misreads an instrument and types the wrong value has still made a transcription error. A QA head will catch “cannot” immediately.
  • “The parameter data still lives on paper.” Wrong in a way any process engineer would notice. Process parameters live in control systems and historians. What paper holds is execution context: setpoints actually used, material lots, operator actions.
  • “100% content fidelity.” It's an absolute claim, and the first customer to find one dropped note discredits the rep and the product. We described the verification mechanism instead.
  • An approval step described as “replaced with a checkbox.” To a Quality reader, that sounds like a shortcut around electronic-signature rules. We reworded it to say exactly what is recorded.
  • Even a data project. When I built a classifier that sorts CRM contacts into teams by job title, the expert overturned exclusions that looked obvious to a marketer.

None of these are hype. Every one would pass a brand-voice check, and every one would have cost us credibility with the people we are trying to sell to.

Making “mandatory” actually mandatory

Writing “all content must pass expert review” into your instructions is a good start. It's also a rule a busy agent will occasionally skip. So the gate exists in three places.

  1. In the operating instructions. The project's CLAUDE.md defines twelve quality gates. Expert review is Gate 9: customer-facing and sales-facing output isn't final until it passes. Every workflow, from ads and emails to one-pagers and battle cards, ends with the review step.
  2. In deterministic hooks. Claude Code supports hooks, scripts that run automatically on events. I split my rules into two groups:
    • Rules that string matching can enforce are Python. A lint hook blocks banned hype terms and a few category-positioning mistakes before a file is written. Run against every in-scope file in the repo, it blocked three, and all three were genuine problems. A metric hook lists any percentage or dollar figure that isn't in our approved sources, as advice rather than a block.
    • Rules that need judgement go to the expert agent.
  3. In a Stop hook. When Claude tries to end its turn, a script checks whether customer-facing files were written without an SME Review Verdict. If so, the turn can't end until the review happens.

The escape hatches are deliberate. I can type “skip SME” to stand the gate down for a session, and every hook fails open, so a crash never blocks work. Strict where it counts and easy to override on purpose is a better design than strict everywhere.

What the reviewer knows, and what it doesn't

The expert is only as good as its context. It loads the product overview, the approved proof-point library and the competitive landscape on every review, plus capacity or persona material when the content touches those. Its job isn't to know everything from memory. Its job is to check the content against what's true.

It also needs to remember rulings. Sometimes I approve a claim the reviewer would otherwise keep flagging, because leadership has signed off on it or because the edge case it worries about only matters on request. I record that ruling, so the expert doesn't relitigate it every time. Without that, a strict reviewer turns into a noisy one, and a noisy reviewer gets ignored.

To be honest about the limits: this is a model playing an expert. It doesn't replace a real expert's sign-off on high-stakes material, and every asset that is customer facing still goes through a human subject matter expert before publication. What this agent does is raise the floor. The obvious-to-a-buyer mistakes never reach a human reviewer, which makes their time far more valuable.

Building your own

  1. Write the persona from your buyer's side of the table. Give it a career, not a job title, and make it someone your customers would recognise as a peer.
  2. Fence it in. List the marketing feedback it isn't allowed to give.
  3. Give it a fixed output format: severity, exact text, the buyer's objection, a suggested revision and a verdict.
  4. Ground it in a source of truth, with an approved claims library that every metric must trace to.
  5. Enforce it in code. Lint what string matching can catch, and use a hook to stop the agent from finishing without a verdict.
  6. Record your rulings, so strictness doesn't turn into noise.

AI made producing content cheap. It didn't make being right cheap. In a technical market, the agent that protects your credibility is worth more than the ones that produce your copy.
